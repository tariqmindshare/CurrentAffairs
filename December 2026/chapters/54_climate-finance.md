### Climate Finance and Bangladesh: Paying for Survival

English Essay for BCS Written Examination

[ছবি: Embankment along a river with people carrying sandbags]

#### Introduction

Bangladesh contributes very little to global greenhouse gas emissions, yet it is one of the countries most exposed to the impacts of climate change. Its low-lying delta, dense population and dependence on agriculture make it vulnerable to floods, cyclones, river erosion, salinity intrusion and rising sea levels. Adapting to these threats costs money: stronger embankments, cyclone shelters, salt-tolerant crops, climate-proof roads and support for displaced people. The question of who pays, and how, lies at the heart of the debate on climate finance. For Bangladesh, climate finance is not a matter of charity; it is a matter of justice and survival.

#### What Is Climate Finance?

Climate finance refers to local, national or international funding, drawn from public, private and alternative sources, that supports action to address climate change. It has three broad purposes:

- **Mitigation:** reducing greenhouse gas emissions, for example through solar power, energy efficiency and cleaner transport.
- **Adaptation:** reducing vulnerability to climate impacts, for example through embankments, early warning systems and resilient housing.
- **Loss and damage:** responding to harm that cannot be avoided through adaptation, such as land permanently lost to the sea.

#### The Global Framework

The principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities" was set out in the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), adopted in 1992. It recognises that developed countries, which have emitted the most historically, should take the lead and support developing countries.

- **Copenhagen (COP15, 2009):** Developed countries pledged to mobilise USD 100 billion a year for developing countries by 2020.
- **Cancun (COP16, 2010):** The Green Climate Fund (GCF) was established. Its headquarters is in Songdo, South Korea.
- **Paris (COP21, 2015):** The Paris Agreement aimed to keep global warming well below 2°C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.
- **Dubai (COP28, 2023):** The Fund for responding to Loss and Damage was operationalised.
- **Baku (COP29, 2024):** Countries agreed a New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG): developed countries are to take the lead in mobilising at least USD 300 billion a year by 2035, with a wider call to scale up finance from all sources to USD 1.3 trillion a year.

#### Bangladesh's Needs

Bangladesh has prepared detailed plans to face climate change. The Bangladesh Climate Change Strategy and Action Plan (BCCSAP) was adopted in 2009. The National Adaptation Plan (NAP) 2023-2050 identifies 113 interventions and estimates that around USD 230 billion will be needed over 27 years, roughly USD 8.5 billion a year. According to the country's third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0), annual adaptation needs could rise to USD 12-14 billion by 2035. The Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100, approved in 2018, takes a long-term view of water, land and disaster management.

#### Domestic Efforts

Bangladesh did not wait for foreign money. It created the Bangladesh Climate Change Trust Fund (BCCTF) from its own budget, backed by the Climate Change Trust Act 2010. The country also introduced climate budget tracking to show how much public spending is related to climate. Community-based adaptation, floating gardens, raised homesteads, cyclone preparedness volunteers and early warning systems have earned international praise. In many respects, Bangladesh is seen as a global leader in adaptation and disaster risk reduction.

#### The Gap and the Challenges

Despite these efforts, a large gap remains between needs and the money received.

- **Inadequate volume:** International flows fall far short of the estimated needs.
- **Loans instead of grants:** A large share of climate finance comes as loans. This adds to the debt burden of a country that did not cause the crisis.
- **Complex access:** Funds such as the GCF have lengthy accreditation and approval procedures that many local institutions find difficult.
- **Bias towards mitigation:** Globally, more money goes to mitigation projects than to adaptation, though adaptation is the main need of Bangladesh.
- **Governance concerns:** Weak monitoring, delays and allegations of misuse in some projects reduce donor confidence.
- **Little reaches the local level:** Communities on the front line, such as those in the coastal belt, often receive only a small part of the money.

#### The Way Forward

- Bangladesh should press, together with other Least Developed Countries and climate-vulnerable nations, for grant-based adaptation and loss-and-damage finance.
- National institutions need stronger capacity to prepare bankable projects and to gain direct access to international funds.
- Transparency must be improved through independent audits, public reporting and digital tracking of climate spending.
- Private investment can be attracted to renewable energy, green bonds and climate-resilient infrastructure.
- Locally led adaptation should be prioritised so that money reaches the people who need it most.

#### Conclusion

Climate change is a global problem caused mostly by others, but its heaviest burden falls on countries like Bangladesh. Fair, adequate and accessible climate finance is therefore an obligation, not a favour. Bangladesh has shown that it can make good use of limited resources. If the world keeps its promises and Bangladesh strengthens transparency and local delivery, climate finance can help turn this vulnerable delta into a model of resilience.
